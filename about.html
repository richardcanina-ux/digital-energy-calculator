About the numbers

Every number in the calculator lives in one file, data/coefficients.js, next to its source. This page reads from that same file, so the numbers here always match the calculator.

These are estimates for a typical day, not measurements of your devices. We picked a middle value from published research for each factor and list the range researchers report.

The main idea: hours matter more than quality

You might have seen headlines saying one hour of streaming uses as much energy as boiling several kettles. Those numbers multiply the data you download by an average "energy per gigabyte." With that method, one hour of HD video would use about Wh.

The International Energy Agency and the Carbon Trust showed that method overstates the network's share. Internet equipment uses most of its power just by being switched on, whether you stream or not. More data adds only a little. So we use their power model: every hour you spend online gets a fair share of the network's always-on power, plus a small extra for the data you move. With it, an hour of HD video comes to about Wh.

That's why changing 4K to HD saves very little in our results, while spending an hour less online saves much more.

The screen matters too. A typical TV uses about W while it's on, about times the network and data centers behind an HD show. So if you watch on a TV, the TV is the biggest part of your video total.

Formulas

"Online Wh per hour" is the network and data-center energy for one hour online:

Online Wh per hour = W (internet lines) + W (home Wi-Fi router) + W × Mbps + Wh (data centers)

Each yearly total turns daily hours into a year: hours per day × 365 × Wh per hour ÷ 1,000 = kWh per year.

The spinning disk on the results card works like the disk in an old electric meter: it turns faster when you use more (within limits: very high use all turns at the same top speed, and very low use at the same slow speed). A real disk turns too slowly to see, so ours is sped up. Its speed is a picture of your average power, not a separate measurement. It spins for a few seconds after your results appear, then stops.

Pledges and tips

Each pledge runs the same formulas again with one habit changed (for example, one less hour of video a day). The saving is the yearly total before the change minus the total after it. The small meter on the pledge step shows your total minus that saving, and the percentage is the saving divided by your total. The tips cover your biggest categories, up to three. If every habit is zero, no tips are shown.

Assumptions and limits

  • No double counting. The electricity that charges your phone, laptop and tablet is counted only under "Charging devices." Streaming, music, social media, studying, working, browsing, calls and phone gaming count only the network and data centers. A TV or monitor is plugged in, so it's never in "Charging devices"; its power is added to video when you watch on it.
  • TVs and monitors are a typical certified model: the median of the ENERGY STAR certified models (about 55-inch TVs and 27-inch monitors). A bigger screen uses more, and a smaller one less. For gaming we assume a console plays on a TV and a PC on one monitor. The desktop computer a monitor is plugged into is not counted, and neither is a monitor used for studying, work or browsing (only video and gaming ask about screens).
  • Home Wi-Fi is assumed. Mobile data networks use different numbers, and we don't model them separately.
  • Data-center energy for video is also used for music, social media, studying, working, browsing, calls and gaming, because we found no separate published figure. Audio probably needs less.
  • Florida's grid is used for all CO₂, even though many data centers that serve you are in other states. Grid losses (about %) are not added.
  • Overlapping activities are allowed. If you listen to music while scrolling, both are counted.
  • Averages hide big differences. A high-end gaming PC, a long AI "reasoning" chat or a 4K TV can use far more than our middle values.
  • No personal data. We never collect names, emails, student IDs or anything that could identify you.

Why this matters

The Story of More, Chapter 11: Moving Around

Hope Jahren remembers when Skype arrived in 2003, and people predicted that face-to-face meetings were finished. Instead, Americans now take almost two million more flights a year than they did in 2003. She tells a similar story about cars. After the 1973 oil embargo, engineers made engines about 50% more efficient by 1980. Americans then switched to minivans, SUVs and pickups, and by 2000 average fuel efficiency was lower than twenty years earlier. Jahren calls this a fork in the road where we chose the Story of More over a Story of Enough.

Digital technology repeats the pattern. Each video call, song or AI answer keeps getting more efficient, yet total data-center electricity is on track to roughly double by 2030. Efficiency alone doesn't lower the total if we keep using more.

The Story of More, Chapter 12: The Plants We Burn

Almost 90% of the energy used on Earth comes from burning coal, oil and natural gas. Jahren warns about how we talk about it:

"the American conversation about oil, gas, and coal is never about how much we use, only about where we can get more."

She makes the point with her friend Brian, who smoked for twenty years. Cigarettes took a smaller share of his salary over time, but the number he smoked each week rose sevenfold. Energy works the same way: fossil fuels fell from 94% to 85% of the world's energy in fifty years, while the total amount burned more than doubled.

That's why this calculator shows your total kWh, not just how efficient each app is. It asks how much we use.

Before the Flood (National Geographic, 2016)

  • The film explains that coal and natural gas generate most of our electricity (about 8:35). In Florida, that mostly means natural gas.
  • It visits Miami Beach, where "sunny-day flooding" pushes seawater up through the storm drains. The city was raising roads and installing pumps in a project of about $400 million, which the mayor said would buy about 40 to 50 years. Florida is described as the most at-risk state for sea-level rise (about 19:40 to 21:40). That is our county.
  • Sunita Narain, of India's Centre for Science and Environment, points out that one American uses as much electricity at home as about 10 people in China, 34 in India or 61 in Nigeria. She argues that lifestyle and consumption must be at the center of climate talks (about 36:15).
  • The film ends by saying we can't know how the story ends, but we can control what we do next: how we live, what we consume and how we vote (about 1:26:30). Our pledge is one small version of that.
